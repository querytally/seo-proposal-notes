Write the SEO proposal for the person who signs

Published by Querytally. We sell research to SEO agencies, so we have an interest in the subject.

The person who asks for a proposal is often not the person who signs it. A marketing manager requests it, reads it closely and forwards it. The owner opens it between two other things, reads the first page, skims the rest and looks for the number at the end.

Most proposals are written for the first reader. They lose with the second.

What the owner is looking for

The owner has one question: does this agency understand my business better than the other two? A list of services does not answer it. Every agency offers audits, content and links. A site score does not answer it either. Owners do not think in scores. They think in customers they did not get.

What answers the question is the owner's own market, on one page. The searches their customers make. How many people make them each month in the US. And, for each, which rival has a page for it while the owner's site does not, with a link the owner can click.

That last part matters more than it looks. An owner may doubt a number. It is hard to doubt a competitor's live page answering a search their own site ignores.

Put the gaps before the price

Order is half the persuasion. A proposal that leads with the price makes the owner judge the cost before seeing what it buys. Put the market first, then the gaps, then the plan, and only then the price and terms.

By the time the owner reaches the number, they should already know which pages month one builds, and why those come first.

Write fresh what only fits this client

Templates are useful, but only for some parts. Terms, reporting dates and the list of what the client provides (access, answers, photos, someone to approve pages) can be the same every time.

The market, the gaps and the plan cannot. Three proposals on an owner's desk look alike unless one holds their own searches, their own rivals and the pages they are missing. That is the part they remember after the call.

People clearly look for shortcuts here. About 140 US searches a month are for "seo proposal template", close to the 170 for "seo proposal" itself. A template saves time on the parts that do not decide the deal. It cannot write the part that does.

Say what you will not promise

Rankings, traffic and dates are not yours to give. Saying so in writing reads as confidence, not caution. It also protects month four, when the client compares what happened with what the proposal said.

Make the deliverables checkable instead. "Ongoing optimisation" cannot be checked. "Twelve new service pages in three months" can.

Keep it short enough to finish

The owner should be able to read all of it. For most small clients that means about two pages for the market, the gaps and the plan, with the terms after. If a section would not change the owner's decision, cut it or move it to an appendix.

After the signature

The gap rows do not expire when the deal closes. They become the first month's work and the baseline for the first monthly report. The owner signed because of them, so it makes sense that the work starts there.
